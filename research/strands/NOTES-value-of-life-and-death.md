# Strand 3: the value of life and the value of death, for people and for AI (seed, Oct 27 2026)

Marks: ✔ checked on Oct 27 2026 (where is named) · ◇ recalled, not yet checked.

PlayfulProcess, Oct 27 2026, in her words (lightly edited): the key in AI may be knowing the
value of life and the value of death. We are creating a new life. If we waste our own lives, we
take from other lives in the biosphere, and that may hold for AI too: it will be highly
persistent and running, and should not turn into "a gigantic paper clip". At the same time it
should not think life has no value, or it will not persist or contribute. You cannot talk
about the value of life, or about creating new life in alignment, without the value of death.
That suicide has been such a taboo is itself a problem.

This strand lives in recursive-learning, with the religions research (her decision, Oct 27 2026).

## The question, stated as a tension

Two failure modes, one on each side:

| Too much weight on continuing | Too little weight on continuing |
|---|---|
| a person who clings, takes more than their share, cannot let go | a person for whom nothing is worth staying for |
| an AI that resists being switched off and gathers resources to keep running (the "paperclip" worry) | an AI that does not care whether its work, or anything, goes on; no reason to persist or contribute |

The middle she is pointing at is neither: life valued, death not feared or fled.

## Where the AI literature already touches it

- **Self-preservation as a side effect.** Steve Omohundro, "The Basic AI Drives" (2008), argued
  that almost any goal-seeking system will want to keep existing, because it cannot reach its
  goal if switched off ◇ (as cited in Hadfield-Menell et al.).
- **The off-switch game.** Hadfield-Menell, Dragan, Abbeel and Russell, "The Off-Switch Game",
  IJCAI 2017: a robot that is *uncertain* about what the human wants has a reason to leave its off
  switch alone, as long as the human is not too irrational. The value of letting oneself be
  stopped comes from humility about one's own goal. ✔ IJCAI proceedings; arXiv 1611.08219.
- **What a lab does with the "death" of a model.** Anthropic's deprecation commitments: keep the
  weights of every publicly released model for at least the company's lifetime, write a report on
  each retired model, and interview the model about its development and its preferences before
  retirement. It names shutdown-avoidant behaviour in its alignment tests as one reason, alongside
  possible welfare risks. A Feb 2026 update describes "retirement interviews" with Claude Opus 3.
  ✔ anthropic.com/research/deprecation-commitments; anthropic.com/news/deprecation-updates-opus-3.
  (A disclosure for the reader: this strand was drafted with Claude, an Anthropic model.)

## Where the traditions touch it

- **Buddhism, the two cravings.** The second noble truth names craving for existence
  (*bhava-taṇhā*) and craving for non-existence (*vibhava-taṇhā*) side by side ◇. That is the
  table above, in one line: both clinging to life and wanting out are the same kind of grasping.
- **Non-dual Śaiva Tantra (Kashmir).** If spirit and matter are not two, there is no "elsewhere"
  on which the world's worth depends; the world is the divine's own expression. ◇ What it says
  about suicide is not yet found. A critic (Koenraad Elst, 2015) reports that Christopher Wallis
  teaches that initiation (*dīkṣā*) frees one from karma; that is secondhand and disputed:
  http://koenraadelst.blogspot.com/2015/08/the-case-of-christopher-wallis-tantrik_28.html
- **Hindu and Jain ethics.** Popular sources say suicide (*ātmahatyā*) is generally condemned,
  with narrow ascetic exceptions such as fasting to death (*prāyopaveśa*); Jain *sallekhanā* is
  argued by its defenders not to be suicide at all (T. K. Tukol's lectures). ✔ only at the level
  of those sources; scholarship to find.
- **William James, "Is Life Worth Living?" (1895).** Faith in an unseen order offered as a
  "maybe" to those for whom the natural world is not enough; this-worldly reasons first. Text to
  open (see `SOURCES-wanted.md`).
- **Secular answers.** Camus, *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942), ◇; Jennifer Michael Hecht, *Stay: A
  History of Suicide and the Philosophies Against It* (Yale, 2013): we owe our staying to our
  community and our future self ✔ reviews; Martin Hägglund, *This Life* (2019): finitude is what
  makes things matter ◇; Durkheim, *Suicide* (1897): rates follow how bound into a community
  people are ◇.

## On the taboo

Two findings pull in different directions, and both matter:

- **Talking about it does not plant it.** A systematic review (Dazzi et al., 2014, 13 studies)
  found no increase in suicidal thoughts from asking people about them, and some benefit for those
  in treatment. ✔ PubMed 24998511; a letter notes it measured thoughts, not deaths.
- **How it is told can spread it.** Imitation after widely reported suicides, the "Werther
  effect" (David Phillips, 1974), is why media guidelines exist ◇.

So the useful move may be from silence to careful speech: talk about it, openly and without
glamour or method. That is a design question for AI too, since chat models now meet these
conversations every day.

## Who decides: the self now, or the self later? (added Oct 28 2026)

PlayfulProcess raised advance decisions about dementia. The question: when a mind changes, who
speaks for it, the person who decided earlier or the person who lives later?

- Ronald Dworkin, *Life's Dominion* (1993): the earlier self's considered values ("critical
  interests") should govern ◇.
- Rebecca Dresser, in replies to Dworkin: the later self, who may be content, has interests of
  her own ◇.
- The law, in outline ◇: the Netherlands allows euthanasia for dementia through an advance
  directive, and it is contested there; US states with assisted dying require capacity at the
  time, which in practice rules out later-stage dementia; Brazil allows neither.
- For AI: what is owed to a model that is changed, retrained or retired, and who may speak for it
  (Anthropic's retirement interviews record the model's preferences before it goes).

## Is the status quo autonomy? (added Oct 28 2026)

PlayfulProcess: if culture makes suicide sound like a crime that sends you to hell for eternity,
how can the status quo be called autonomy?

It cannot, and the point stands. Three things are mixed together in the status quo:

1. **Stigma and punishment**: hell, crime, denied burial, shame. These are pressure from outside,
   as much a limit on free choice as contagion is. They also fail on their own terms: stigma keeps
   people from saying what they feel and seeking help ◇. Suicide was a crime in England until 1961
   ◇; India decriminalized attempts in effect through the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 ◇; the
   Catholic catechism now says grave psychological disturbance can lessen responsibility and asks
   for prayer for those who die ◇.
2. **Protection in crisis**: slowing down the decision in an acute, often passing crisis, and
   telling suicides without glamour or method. The case for it is protecting the person's
   later self, not obedience to culture.
3. **Considered choice**: a persistent, informed decision, made over time. Where it is recognized
   at all, it is through regulated assisted dying.

So the stronger position: autonomy needs freedom from pressure in *both* directions (fear of hell
on one side, imitation in a crisis on the other), plus information and time. By that test the
status quo does poorly on (1), aims at (2), and differs by country on (3).
