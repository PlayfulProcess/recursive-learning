# The Roman rhyme, wider than the digital (Oct 27 2026)

Continues `NOTES-roman-rhyme.md`. PlayfulProcess, Oct 27 2026: go beyond the digital world, to
polarization, populism, corruption, lack of trust, the turn against globalization, people sorting
by geography along political identity, and the barbarian invasions read as cybersecurity moving
from prevention to resilience. Narrative and heuristic are enough here.

Marks: ✔ checked on Oct 27 2026 (where is named) · ◇ recalled, not yet checked.

## Two warnings first, both from historians

1. **The rhyme can fit anything.** Alexander Demandt, *Der Fall Roms* (1984), collected 210
   causes that scholars have given for the fall, some contradicting others ("totalitarianism" and
   "excessive freedom" both appear). ✔ University of Washington course page reproducing the list
   from Demandt p. 695; a 2014 second edition added more. A rhyme table is a lens, never a proof.
2. **"Decline and fall" is itself a political tool.** Edward J. Watts, *The Eternal Decline and
   Fall of Rome: The History of a Dangerous Idea* (Oxford, 2021), traces how the story has been
   used from Cato the Elder to today by politicians who promise renewal, and the people
   scapegoated in its name. ✔ OUP page and reviews. So the rhyme belongs inside the populism row
   below, not only outside it: telling people "we are Rome falling" is one of the moves populists
   make.

## The rhyme table

| Theme | Then | Now | Where it breaks | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fragmentation of the centre** | Crisis of the Third Century (235-284): more than 20 emperors, most made by their armies and killed; the Gallic Empire (260-274) and Palmyra (c. 267-272) broke away and were taken back | secession talk, regions governing apart from the capital, "states' rights" fights; the EU and the UK parting | Rome's breakaways were reabsorbed within about 15 years; Diocletian rebuilt the state | ✔ Wikipedia, World History Encyclopedia (counts differ by source) |
| **Populism and the army's acclaim** | emperors raised by soldiers' acclamation and by donatives (cash gifts); the grain dole and games in the city (*panem et circenses*, Juvenal) | leaders raised by direct appeal over the heads of parties, the press and courts; rallies and feeds as the arena | voters are not legions; elections still change hands | ◇ |
| **Polarization by identity** | circus factions, the Blues and the Greens, in Constantinople; in the Nika riot of 532 they united against Justinian, who crushed them; fierce Christian doctrinal splits (Arian and Nicene, the Donatists in Africa) | affective polarization: party as identity, sport-like loyalty, each side's own media | historians argue whether the factions were political at all: Alan Cameron, *Circus Factions* (1976), read them as closer to sports hooligans | ✔ the riot and Cameron's thesis, Antigone Journal and summaries; doctrinal splits ◇ |
| **Corruption as privatized government** | Ramsay MacMullen, *Corruption and the Decline of Rome* (Yale, 1988): state aims were steadily thwarted for private gain by officials and commanders; offices bought and favours sold | regulatory capture, lobbying, revolving doors, state contracts as private income | modern states have audit, a free press and courts that Rome lacked, though each can be captured | ✔ the thesis, Yale catalogue and summaries; the mechanics (*suffragium*) ◇ |
| **Trust in money** | the silver coin (the antoninianus) fell from mostly silver to a few percent between about 238 and 268; Aurelian's reform (c. 274) restored a little; Diocletian replaced it (c. 293) and issued the Edict on Maximum Prices (301) | inflation after 2021, distrust of central banks, crypto as "exit" from state money | modern central banks can and did bring inflation down | ✔ coin figures, CoinWeek and RIC; the Edict date ◇ |
| **Trust in institutions** | (no surveys) | the 2025 Edelman Trust Barometer, "Trust and the Crisis of Grievance": six in ten report a moderate to high sense of grievance; low-income respondents trust institutions 13 points less than high-income ones (48 vs 61) | the Roman side has no data of this kind; this row is the modern half only | ✔ Edelman press release, Jan 2025 (their own survey) |
| **The turn against globalization** | Mediterranean shipwrecks, a proxy for sea trade, peak between about 200 BC and AD 200 and fall after (A. J. Parker's catalogue of 1,189 wrecks, 1992); later, the Pirenne thesis (*Mohammed and Charlemagne*, 1937) argued the decisive break in Mediterranean trade came with the Islamic conquests, not the barbarians | trade fell from about 25% of world GDP (2008) to about 21% (2019); "slowbalisation", tariffs, reshoring, friend-shoring | wrecks record heavy, durable cargo only; whether today is deglobalization or only a slower pace is argued (CEPR's Vox series) | ✔ wrecks: Wilson 2011, Robinson 2020; trade share: Kühne Center 2022; Pirenne ◇ |
| **Sorting by place** | cities shrank behind new walls (Rome's Aurelian Walls, 270s); the rich moved to rural villas; regions drifted apart in language, law and coin | Bill Bishop, *The Big Sort* (2008): the share of Americans in "landslide" counties (a 20-point margin or more) rose from 26.8% (1976) to 48.3% (2004); Tim Urban (Wait But Why), *What's Our Problem?* (2023), on tribal "low-rung" politics | Bishop's data are contested: Abrams and Fiorina, using voter registration, found sorting falling; Urban's book is a framework, not a data study | ✔ Bishop and the critique, Prospect, Wilson Quarterly, Hoover; Urban, publisher pages; walls and villas ◇ |
| **Elites withdraw** | senatorial landowners lived on fortified estates and avoided tax; patrons took in small farmers (*patrocinium*, see the first note) | gated communities, private cities, bunkers, the "network state" (Balaji Srinivasan, *The Network State*, 2022: like-minded communities leaving existing states to found their own) | modern elites still need the state's courts, currency and armies | ✔ Srinivasan, Wikipedia and news; the Roman half ◇ |
| **Private force** | *bucellarii*, private retainers of generals and aristocrats, sworn to their employer; Belisarius had over 1,000 in North Africa (533, Procopius) | private military firms, private security outnumbering police in many countries, corporate security teams | modern private force is licensed by states, mostly | ✔ the bucellarii, Wikipedia, Osprey; "outnumbering police" ◇ |
| **Plague and climate** | the Antonine plague (from c. 165), the plague of Cyprian (c. 249-262), the plague of Justinian (541); the Late Antique Little Ice Age (536 to about 660), after volcanic eruptions in 536, 540 and 547 (Büntgen et al., *Nature Geoscience*, 2016); Kyle Harper, *The Fate of Rome* (2017) | COVID-19; warming, which runs the other way but strains in similar places (harvests, migration, state budgets) | modern medicine and science; the authors themselves urge caution linking climate to politics | ✔ Büntgen 2016 (paper and press); the plague dates ◇ |
| **Barbarian invasions as security** | Edward Luttwak, *The Grand Strategy of the Roman Empire* (1976): a shift from "preclusive" defence at the frontier to "defence in depth" in the 3rd and 4th centuries, with the frontier assumed breakable and mobile field armies behind it | cybersecurity moving from the perimeter (keep them out) to zero trust (NIST SP 800-207, Aug 2020: no implicit trust from network location; the network treated as already compromised) and to resilience (NIST CSF 2.0, Feb 2024, adds Govern beside Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover) | Benjamin Isaac, *The Limits of Empire* (1990), and Susan Mattern, *Rome and the Enemy* (1999), argue Rome had no grand strategy at all; the rhyme may be Luttwak's, not Rome's | ✔ Luttwak and Isaac, Wikipedia and BMCR 1990; NIST 800-207 abstract; CSF 2.0, CSO Online |
| **Hiring the outside in** | barbarian groups settled as *foederati*, fighting for Rome under their own leaders; the Goths admitted across the Danube in 376, then the defeat at Adrianople in 378 | hiring former attackers as defenders; bug bounties; states tolerating criminal hacker groups that serve them | **the trap:** "barbarians at the gates" is a stock far-right image for migrants. Guy Halsall, *Barbarian Migrations and the Roman West* (2007), argued the fall produced the migrations rather than the reverse, and warned that the Roman-versus-barbarian picture is used for anti-immigration politics | ✔ Halsall's argument, Cambridge page and quotations; 376 and 378 ◇ |
| **Keepers of meaning** | Christianity's rise; monasteries; new authorities (bishops) taking civic roles | institutional religion falling in many countries; new meaning-makers (wellness, online teachers, AI as a counsellor) | ◇ to source | ◇ |

## A note on the barbarian row

Keep the rhyme on the **frontier**, not on **people**. The useful parallel is the change in how
defence is thought about: from a wall that is supposed to hold, to an assumption that it will be
crossed and that what matters is what holds after. In this table, "barbarians" means the threat
model, never migrants. That is also what Halsall's warning asks for.

## Patterns across the rows (heuristics to test)

1. **Centre weakens, protection privatizes.** The fragmentation, private force, elites withdraw and
   patronage rows are one movement seen from four sides.
2. **Trust leaves the abstract and goes to the near.** Coin to land, empire to patron, institution to
   tribe. Today: from institutions to people one knows or follows (the Edelman gap).
3. **Defence moves inward.** From the frontier to the depth; from the perimeter to the account.
4. **Decline stories are part of the decline.** Watts: whoever tells the fall story is often
   selling a renewal.
