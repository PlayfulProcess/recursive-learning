    The two rule sets, in our words

    The landlord’s way · after the 1904 patent

    • The player with the least money goes first. Roll two dice and move. Passing (or landing on) Mother Earth pays wages: $100, and counts a lap.
    • Buy an empty lot, railroad or franchise you land on: its price goes into the Public Treasury. Others who land there pay you: lot rent, a fare that grows with the railroads you own, or four times their roll at a franchise.
    • Own three lots on one side and their rent doubles.
    • Absolute necessities cost $20, paid to the Public Treasury, which does nothing with it.
    • A luxury: buy a ticket for $50 (worth $60 at the end) or lose your next turn.
    • No Trespassing sends you to Jail. Each turn there, roll: doubles free you; otherwise pay $50 and move, or stay. The third roll without doubles pays the fine.
    • If you cannot pay, sell land back to the bank at half price; you choose which (the computer gives up the least land value that covers the debt). If that is not enough, you go to the Poorhouse and are out.
    • When every player still in has been round five times (or one player is left), the richest wins: cash, land at its price, and $60 a ticket.

    Prosperity · the single-tax rules, later named Prosperity

    • Same board, same dice, same wages, same Jail; the price of a lot goes into the Public Treasury, as in the landlord’s way.
    • Ground rent goes to the Public Treasury, not to the owner: land is taxed at its value, the single tax.
    • A luxury is $50 into the Treasury, with no ticket (only cash counts), or lose your next turn.
    • Railroads and franchises are public: a $10 fare to the Treasury.
    • When anyone puts a new lot into use, every other player gains $10.
    • Each time a player passes Mother Earth, the Treasury pays half of what it holds to all players, equally.
    • The Poorhouse is a waiting room: the player's land goes back to the bank; they still share in the Treasury's payouts and leave, from Mother Earth, as soon as they hold $50 again.
    • Everyone wins together when the player who started with the least has doubled it and holds at least as much as anyone, while nobody holds less than they started with: the poorest rises to the top, and nobody falls. With equal starts every player must double: the harder table. If every player has been round five times first, the table loses: talk about why.

    The 1904 patent gives the landlord’s rules: the price of land paid into the Public Treasury, the $60 luxury ticket, Jail by doubles or a $50 fine, the end when the last player has passed Mother Earth the fifth time. The 1924 patent lets a player short of cash choose what to sell. Prosperity’s win starts from the one reported by Ketcham (Harper’s, 2012) and Pilon (The Monopolists, 2015), the poorest player doubling; its other two parts (as much as anyone, nobody below their start) are ours, added in October 2026 so the table can lose. The exact amounts here are ours, tuned so a landlord’s game takes about forty minutes and a Prosperity game less.

    The story

    Lizzie Magie applied for a patent on The Landlord’s Game in 1903; it was granted on January 5, 1904 (US 748,626). She meant it as “a practical demonstration of the present system of land-grabbing with all its usual outcomes and consequences”, in her words as The Guardian quotes them. Georgists in her circle published it in 1906; Parker Brothers turned it down in 1909. Her revision was patented in 1924 (US 1,509,312) and published in 1932 as The Landlord’s Game and Prosperity. Charles Darrow sold his version as Monopoly to Parker Brothers in 1935; that year she sold them her patent for $500. In 1936 she disputed his claim in public. She died in 1948.

    The question for the table: after one game each way, which felt more exciting, and why? Her monopolist rules won the shelves. Was it the rules, the marketing, or something in us?
