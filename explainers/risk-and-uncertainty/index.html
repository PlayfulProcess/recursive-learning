Explainer · working draftRisk or uncertainty?

The coin, the urn you can't see into, and the yarrow. In The Fork, played as a tree or as six lines, you can flip a coin at questions nobody can answer yet. This page is about what that coin can and can't do.

Every number here is the odds of a device (a coin, a bowl, an urn we wrote), a count of casts, a toy number said to be one, or a fact about coins, tests or history, with its source. None of them is a chance about AI. A short draft, still to be carved.

Two questions, not one

  1. Do we have odds we could defend? Yes: risk. No: uncertainty.
  2. Is the not knowing in the world, like when a radioactive atom will decay (aleatory, from the Latin for dice), or in us, a gap in what we know, like the order of a shuffled deck (epistemic, from the Greek for knowledge)?

They cut differently. A coin toss is physics we can't track, so its not knowing is mostly in us. Yet it is the textbook case of risk, because its odds are known.

All sections

1 · The short versionRisk, uncertainty, and a coin

  • Risk: you don't know what will happen, but you know the odds.
  • Uncertainty: you don't know the odds either, and maybe nobody can.
  • A fair coin is risk. The questions on the tree are uncertainty.

A coin can end the choosing. It finds nothing out about the world; at most it shows you what you hoped for.

Where people disagree

Many decision theorists follow Frank Ramsey (1926) and Leonard Savage (1954): a consistent person acts as if she had odds for everything. On that view the line between risk and uncertainty is about how far you trust your numbers, not whether numbers exist.

From the other side, Sven Ove Hansson points out that outside textbook dice and coins, almost nothing is pure risk.

2 · The coinA coin is risk

You can't say how the next flip lands, but you can say heads comes up about 1 time in 2. Frank Knight (1921) called this measurable kind of not knowing risk, and kept uncertainty for cases with no odds to measure. John Maynard Keynes, the same year, argued that some probabilities can't be numbers at all. In 1937, writing of a war or of prices twenty years ahead, he put it plainly: “We simply do not know.”

Even a coin is mostly our not knowing how it was launched. Diaconis, Holmes and Montgomery (2007) predicted that a flipped coin lands on the side it started about 51 times in 100. Bartoš and colleagues (2023) counted 350,757 flips and found just under 51 in 100.

Heads is the green part of the bar. These flips use your browser's random numbers, set by us to 1 in 2. A tally shows the device, not the world.

Where people disagree

What Knight meant is itself disputed. LeRoy and Singell (1987) read him as saying people always form their own odds, and that his uncertainty is where insurance can't be bought. Langlois and Cosgel (1993) answer that this reading is too narrow.

Whether a coin in a world of plain physics has any real chance at all is argued over too (SEP, “Chance versus Randomness”). And the line between chance in the world and gaps in us moves with the model: engineers Der Kiureghian and Ditlevsen (2009) call a not knowing epistemic when the modeller sees a way to shrink it.

3 · The urnThe urn you can't see into

Daniel Ellsberg (1961) set out two urns. Urn A holds 50 red balls and 50 black. Urn B holds 100 red and black balls, in a mix you aren't told. Win on red: which urn? Many people pick A. Win on black: many pick A again. But if you picked A for red, you must think B has fewer reds, so more blacks. Picking A twice isn't a guess about B. It is steering away from not knowing the odds. Ellsberg called that ambiguity.

(An urn, here, is just a jar of balls.) Both urns on this page were written by us: any urn a program draws from has odds someone wrote. The tree's urn is stranger. Nobody can count its balls, even afterwards, and we draw once.

Where people disagree

Ellsberg thought the A-and-A pattern can be reasonable. Strict Bayesians call it a mistake, since it fits no single set of odds for urn B. Imprecise-probability theorists model it with a range of odds instead of one number. Some experiments find the aversion less common than the classic story says (SEP, “Imprecise Probabilities”).

4 · Cutting the question“Even odds” depend on how you cut the question

Knowing nothing, it's tempting to say “then it's 1 in 2”. Keynes named this the principle of indifference (1921) and showed its trap: cut the same question another way and the equal odds change. Joseph Bertrand (1889) built a famous puzzle on it.

The Fork's gate is a live case. Ask “once it's out, would it be out for good?” and “would the first real test be the step itself?” as two coins, and the gate comes up yes 1 time in 4. Ask it as one coin: 1 in 2. Same world, same ignorance, different cuts, different “even odds”. It is one of the clearest reasons to doubt that a coin is a good picture of not knowing.

Where people disagree

E. T. Jaynes (1973) argued that the symmetries of a well-posed problem pick out one right cut, and so settle Bertrand's puzzle. Others reply that most real questions, the tree's included, come with no agreed symmetries, so the cut stays a choice. Many Bayesians drop indifference and accept that different people may start from different odds.

5 · Given what?Every chance is a chance given something

A base rate is how common something is before you look at this case. Toy numbers: a condition that 1 in 1,000 people have, and a test that catches every case but wrongly flags 5 in 100 healthy people. You test positive. Picture 1,000 people: about 1 real case, and about 50 healthy people flagged. So about 1 real case among about 51 flagged. Skipping the base rate is a common error (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974).

Many would say every probability is given something, and the “something” is often left unsaid. Which group you count a case among is the reference class problem (Hájek, 2007): another group, another honest number. For a new kind of thing there may be no group to count.

Where people disagree

Hájek argues the reference class problem is everyone's problem, Bayesians included, since starting odds also come from somewhere. Frequentists and Bayesians disagree about who suffers from it most. This page gives no number for the tree: a toy that turned invented causes into leaf counts would be a forecast in costume.

6 · Tested in time?Can “it's safe” be tested in time?

Karl Popper (1959) asked of a claim: what observation could show it wrong? If none could, it isn't doing the work of science. A second trap is induction. Bertrand Russell's chicken (1912) is fed every day and grows sure of it, until the day its neck is wrung.

Counting helps a little. If something went fine N times out of N, the usual upper bound on how often it fails is about 3 in N: the rule of three (Hanley and Lippman-Hand, 1983). The bound is a promise about the method (used again and again, it would cover the true rate 95 times in 100), not the chance that this rate is below 3 in N; that would need starting odds, a prior. It holds only if those N were the same kind of thing as the next one, and with N = 0 there is no bound at all. If the first real test is the step itself, “it's safe” is checked only by taking it.

If the first real test is the step itself, N is 0 for the step that matters.

Where people disagree

The bound uses the usual 95-in-100 standard, a claim about the method's track record. Reading it as “95 in 100 that the rate is below 3 in N” needs a prior, and Bayesians and frequentists disagree about when that step is fair. Many philosophers doubt that falsifiability alone marks off science (SEP, “Karl Popper”).

A subtler worry is the anthropic shadow (Ćirković, Sandberg and Bostrom, 2010): we can only look back from worlds where the worst didn't happen, so a clean record may say less than it seems. That argument is debated.

7 · Who has to showWho has to show what

A burden of proof decides which side wins while the evidence is unclear. Precaution comes in two strengths. The weak form, the Rio Declaration (1992, Principle 15): where harm could be serious or irreversible, lack of full scientific certainty is no reason to put off cost-effective measures against it. “Cost-effective” is part of what keeps it weak. The strong form, the Wingspread Statement (1998): whoever proposes the activity bears the burden of proof.

The Fork's gate is a narrower, conditional version of the strong form: it flips the burden only when a step can't be undone or tried first (Wingspread has no such condition). Then whoever takes it has to show it's safe, and “not shown” counts as “no”. A coin's yes wasn't shown, and neither was a coin's “it can be undone”: a cast can't switch the caution off. Cass Sunstein (2005) answers that stopping has risks too; on the tree, that is the race.

The gate: can't undo it, can't try it first?
Alignment: can we give it our goals, and check it worked, in time?
Containment: can we keep it where it can't get out, even if it tried?

Where people disagree

Sunstein argues the strong form can forbid everything, since every option, stopping included, carries risk. Defenders reply that irreversibility breaks the tie.

Reading “not shown” as “no”, and a coin's yes as not shown, is this page's reading of the tree, not a settled rule.

8 · RuinWhen the average misleads

Some losses end the game, with no next try to learn from. Then an average across many players can mislead any one player. A toy, with toy numbers: each round, 1 time in 20 you lose everything; otherwise your stake grows by half. Across many players the average stake grows every round. Play 50 rounds yourself, though, and you're still in only about 8 times in 100. Ole Peters (2019) calls this the ergodicity problem: the average across people is not what happens to one person over time.

Toy numbers. Nothing here is staked, and nothing is about AI.

Where people disagree

Many economists answer that ordinary decision theory already handles ruin, for example by valuing each doubling of wealth the same, and that the ergodicity framing adds less than it claims. That ruin matters when one try can end the game is widely agreed; how to model it is contested. See also reversibility in Words.

9 · Either wayWhat holds either way

When you can't close the gap by knowing, you can look for what holds under every answer still possible. Planners call this robust decision-making (Lempert, Popper and Bankes, 2003): instead of betting on one forecast, look for moves that do acceptably across many futures. An older cousin is minimax regret (Savage, 1951): pick the option whose worst regret is smallest.

In The Fork, “What holds either way” casts nothing. It lists the dangers every leaf still possible would face. That compares dangers, not moves: one move can aim at dangers under several leaves at once, and the list of moves is still being checked, so “nothing shared” does not mean no move holds either way. When the game prints uncertainty: no known mechanism, it means, on this page: there are no odds that most people would accept (some decision theorists, above, would still give their own), and no lever is known to work.

Where people disagree

Robust choices can be timid, and what counts as “acceptable” is a choice of values, not a finding. Minimax regret depends on which options you list: adding one can change the pick. Both still ask you to say what the possible worlds are.

10 · The questionIs the coin a good metaphor for uncertainty?

As a picture of the tree's not knowing: no. As a way to stop choosing: sometimes, if it says that is all it does.

As a model of uncertainty, a coin flatters:

  • Its odds are known.
  • It can be flipped again.
  • It can't ruin you.
  • Nobody's beliefs change it. The race can be changed by beliefs: believing “they will” can make it truer, a self-fulfilling prophecy (Merton, 1948).

And it has a flaw of its own: its “even odds” depend on how the question was cut (cutting the question).

As a way to stop deliberating, it can be honest. The folk trick: flip, and notice what you hoped for. That is the game's “Glad or sorry?”. There are other honest ways out of a choice you can't settle: find out more where you can (a test, a small trial first), wait if waiting is safe, or look for what holds either way.

Where people disagree

A Bayesian might say a coin is a fine stand-in for your own 1-in-2 belief; the reply is that the cut decides what 1 in 2 means. Lotteries are also used on purpose where fairness matters more than choosing well, as in a tie-break: there the coin's blindness is the point, not a flaw.

11 · The traditionWhat yarrow was for

In early China, tortoise shells and yarrow stalks were mostly used to foretell and to decide. A counter-current treated casting as a way to give a decision its form. The Great Plan (Hongfan), in its part on examining doubts, sets tortoise and yarrow as voices beside the ruler's own heart, the ministers and the people. Xunzi says a ruler divines before great matters not to get what he seeks, but as ornament. The Mawangdui “Yao” shows a Confucius who reads the Changes for virtue rather than omens.

The Fork's yarrow bowl borrows only the odds: half yes, half no, and 1 in 4 turning. The odds are the bowl's, not the world's.

Where people disagree

How far that counter-current reached is argued over, and so are the dating of the “Yao” and what Xunzi's word for ornament (wen) covers. Translations differ: check each one before quoting. None of these texts is about AI, and none is a forecast.
