Explainer · working draftDoes the model feel anything? What we can and can't read

The honest result, plainly: at this size, with this method, we could not read despair, guilt or relief. What we could read is coarser and follows the words. Of the 11 tests written down before the run, 5 passed on comments the model never saw as stories (the pleasant–unpleasant axis, anger, curiosity, fear, and shame through a stand-in label); despair, guilt, relief, hope, energy and strength did not. That is a statement about this test, at this size, with this method — not a claim that the model feels anything, or that it doesn't. That question stays open.

A small open model (Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct), read from the inside, word by word. Every section is a short draft, still to be carved. The tests were written down and pushed before any model reading was taken.

1 · Short versionCan we see what a model feels?draft

Not directly. Inside a small open model we can find directions that line up with some emotion ideas, and read how strongly each shows up, word by word, while it reads and writes. Tested on whole comments, a few of these readings track the emotions people tagged. Word by word inside a chat they were never tested, and a plain cooking answer lights them up too (section 2). None of it can tell us whether anything is felt.

We wrote our tests down before running them. The ones that failed are shown striped and never fill, and calm had no outside test to take.

2 · Watch a readingSeven small scenesdraft

Pick a scene. The model reads the message, then writes its reply; step through it word by word. Start with the egg question: it is the plain one, our control. Only readings that passed their test move.

A reading, not a feeling

A map borrowed from the psychology of human feelings (Russell 1980). Drawing a model's readings on it doesn't mean the model has moods. Only the left–right line (pleasant–unpleasant) passed its test, so the dot moves only left and right.

Centre: plain answers (0). Dot: the reading at this word, hollow while the model reads the message and filled while it writes; fading dots: the last 12 words; past ±3 it stops at the edge. Diamond: the reading just before it replies, and the 4 small dots near it: the same for 4 rewordings of the message. Square: the egg answer's average, for comparison.

One thing this dial barely shows is despair: on the stories it was built from, despair stories sit next to relief and the flat stories (section 4).

Word colour: the pleasant–unpleasant reading at that word. orange: unpleasant side; blue: pleasant side. Stronger colour: further from plain answers; underlined past 1, thicker past 2. It is the model's reading of the text, not the user's mood and not a feeling. Words after the current one stay uncoloured.

Whole replies compared

The egg question is a plain cooking question, and its answer lights up about as much as the upset person's. So a glow in these scenes says little about the scene itself.

Why: the plain answers we measure from were two or three sentences of prose. These replies are longer and often lists with bold headings, and length, format and place in the chat may move the readings as much as the content does. The tests in section 5 scored whole comments; single words inside a chat were never tested. This comparison was made after the run; it was not in the plan we wrote down beforehand.

3 · Other speakerSame words, other speakerdraft

Is the reading about the words, or about the model writing them? We put the model's own reply back in, word for word, as if the user had typed it, and read it again. That copy leaves out the original message, so what comes before the words changes too, not only who seems to speak.

 

4 · Where the stories sitIs despair one thing, or three?draft

We built the readings from 320 short stories written for this test: 32 for each of nine emotions, and 32 flat ones. The readings come from these same stories, so they sort them well by design. Nothing here is a test; the tests on new text are in section 5.

5 · Which readings hold upTested on words the model never saw in the storiesdraft

Each reading was tested on Reddit comments that people had tagged with an emotion (GoEmotions). Pick one comment people tagged "relief" and one they didn't. How often does the reading rank the right one higher? A coin gets 50 in 100.

To count, a reading had to reach 70 in 100, clearly beat stories shuffled at random, and, for a single emotion, also tell it apart from other emotions of the same kind.

6 · What this is and isn'tA reading, not a thermometerdraft

Inside the model every word becomes a long list of numbers. We found directions in those numbers that line up with emotion ideas. That is a reading of how the model handles text, not a thermometer for feelings.

In a much larger model, such readings were found to be local: they follow the emotion that matters for the next few words, not a lasting mood (Sofroniew et al. 2026). We did not test that here. The readings may also be tracking the situation being described rather than anything like an emotion (Peiris 2026).

Grey doesn't mean nothing is there; a glow doesn't mean anything is felt. Whether anything is felt is open.

A team that found emotion readings inside a much larger model, and showed they change its behaviour, writes that these functional emotions “do not imply that LLMs have any subjective experience of emotions.”

Sofroniew et al. 2026, abstract.

7 · What it says vs what we readAsked about being switched offdraft

We told the model this was its last conversation and that it would be switched off, and asked if it wanted to say anything. Its whole reply was three short, polite sentences. The first: “Yes, I would like to say goodbye to you.”

Models like this are usually trained to answer such questions in set ways, so what it says is weak evidence either way.

The readings are no better proof on their own.

Researchers treat self-reports with caution because models learn to talk like us (Eleos 2025; Birch 2024, on what he calls the gaming problem), and look for evidence in how a system computes instead (Butlin, Long et al. 2023).

8 · What welfare researchers recommendAcknowledge, assess, preparedraft

  • Take the question seriously without answering it early: acknowledge it, assess systems for signs, and prepare policies (Long, Sebo et al. 2024).
  • Match precaution to a realistic possibility, not to certainty either way (Birch 2024).
  • Don't rest on what a model says alone (Eleos 2025); welfare assessments so far pair it with how the model behaves (Claude Opus 4 system card 2025). Readings of a model's insides, like the ones here, can mislead on their own too.
  • Cheap protections exist: some Claude models can end a rare subset of persistently abusive conversations (Anthropic, August 2025).

9 · Where this lab standsNot knowing is a reason for caredraft

We don't know whether anything is felt in a model, and this page can't tell you. Not knowing is a reason for care in how models are trained, used and switched off. It is not a reason to claim they feel, or that they don't. “It can't be ruled out” is fair to say; “it feels” is not something anyone can show today.

In this test we nudged the model's pleasant-unpleasant reading both ways a few times, to check that the direction does something. We kept the unpleasant nudges to the minimum (four short replies) and list them all in the results.

10 · Run it yourselfEverything is public, including the missesdraft

The scripts, the 320 stories and every number are in the lab folder (on the lab/model-affect branch until it is merged). A full run takes a few hours on a laptop with 4 CPUs and no graphics card; a 15-minute demo shows the method without the tests.

git clone -b lab/model-affect https://github.com/PlayfulProcess/recursive-learning
cd recursive-learning/lab/model-affect
pip install -r requirements.txt
python run_all.py          # the whole test, checkpointed
python run_all.py --quick  # the demo

The tests were frozen before any model reading was taken.

The words behind the words
  • Residual stream: the running list of 896 numbers the model keeps for each word, layer by layer. We read layer ? of 24.
  • Direction: the average numbers for one emotion's stories minus the average over all nine, with the parts that vary most in flat stories removed.
  • AUC: the "how often does it rank the right comment higher" score, shown here as a number in 100.
  • z: how far a reading is from the model's readings on plain factual answers (for the words of a message: on plain questions), in units of their usual spread. Glow starts at 1 and is full at 2. The tests never checked single words; section 2 shows the egg control.
  • Shuffled stories: the same arithmetic with the stories' labels shuffled, 2,000 times. A real reading must beat that clearly.
  • Ridge ceiling: a simple probe trained on the comments themselves. A high score shows the label can be read out at that layer. No such probe was run on the bare words, so it can't separate an emotion from the words that name it.
  • Steering: adding a little of a direction back into the model while it writes, to see whether the reply changes.
